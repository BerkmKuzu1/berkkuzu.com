Virtual FPGA board

Write VHDL for the pins of this board, program it, and play with its switches and buttons while your design runs: LEDs light up, the display counts, and text arrives in the terminal. Start from one of the examples, or from your own code from the VHDL book.

How the board works

Programming compiles your design with the same VHDL-2008 simulator as the book, here in your browser, and connects its ports to the pins of the board by name: a port called led drives the LEDs, a port called sw reads the switches. The list of pins is below the board.

A real board runs at 100 MHz. A simulator in a browser manages a few hundred thousand clock cycles a second for a small design, so this board runs at a clock you choose, from 10 Hz to 1 MHz, and shows how close to real time it gets. Write timing with a generic, CLK_HZ: the board fills in its clock frequency, and the same code runs on a real board with CLK_HZ set to 100 MHz. A BAUD generic gets the speed of the terminal in the same way.

The LEDs and the display show how long each one was lit since the last screen update, as your eye would: a PWM output looks dimmed, and a display that lights its digits in turn looks steady, as long as it switches fast enough. Slow the clock down to watch either happen.

Set Run on to the FPGA and the board runs real hardware instead: your design is built for VF-1, a small FPGA modelled in this page, and the board runs the bitstream on an emulator of that chip. Inside the FPGA, below the board, shows every step.

    Inside the FPGA
    
                
    
                    Here your design becomes hardware. Synthesis turns the VHDL into logic gates and flip-flops, mapping
                    packs the gates into 4-input lookup tables, placement puts those on the tiles of a small FPGA,
                    routing connects them through its wires and switches, and the configuration of every tile and
                    switch is written into a bitstream. Program the board from the bitstream and it runs on an emulator
                    of the chip that reads nothing but those bits.
                
    
                
    
                    How VF-1 is built
                    
      
                        
    • Logic. 24 × 24 tiles. Each holds one 4-input LUT and one flip-flop that takes the rising clock edge
                            and can have an asynchronous set or reset. The flip-flop's input is its own LUT; the LUT output O and the
                            flip-flop output Q both leave the tile.
      • 
                        
    • Routing. Channels of 12 wires run between the tiles, and each wire spans one tile. The switch box at
                            each corner can join wire t to wire t of the other channels there. Every tile input can pick
                            any wire of the four channels around its tile, and every output can drive any of them.
      • 
                        
    • Pins. The 51 pins of the board sit on the ring of I/O tiles, always in the same places: switches and LEDs
                            at the bottom, the display at the top, the buttons on the right, the serial port on the left. The clock
                            reaches every flip-flop through its own network.
      • 
                        
    • Timing. A LUT takes 0.45 ns, a flip-flop 0.35 ns from the clock edge to Q and needs its input 0.15 ns
                            before the edge. A wire with its switch adds 0.35 ns, a tile input 0.15 ns, an input pad 0.6 ns and an
                            output pad 0.8 ns.
      • 
                        
    • Bitstream. 146 bits per tile: the LUT's 16-bit INIT, 4 bits for the flip-flop, a 6-bit multiplexer for each of
                            the 5 inputs and an enable for each of the 48 wires that O and Q can drive. Then 72 bits per switch box and a few
                            per pad: 16,197 bytes, closed by a CRC-32.
      • 
                    
    
                    
    The tools, all in your browser: synthesis reads the design with the simulator's own analyzer and builds an
                        and-inverter graph; mapping enumerates 4-input cuts and picks the fewest levels of LUTs, then fewer LUTs at that
                        depth; placement is simulated annealing on wire length; routing is PathFinder, where nets negotiate for wires
                        that are wanted twice; timing adds up the delays of the routed paths. The emulator rebuilds the circuit from the
                        bits alone: it joins the wires whose switches are on, finds the one output that drives each of those nets,
                        and runs the LUTs and flip-flops clock cycle by clock cycle.
